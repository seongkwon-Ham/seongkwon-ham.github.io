Working paperNov 2026

Tracing Carbon CostsMacroeconomic Impacts of Korean ETS Policy Through the Power Sector

with Soojin Jo and Myungkyu Shim

Abstract

Carbon prices in Korea do not reach the price of electricity yet, as most allowances are free and the retail price is a policy decision. This paper traces the response of the economy to a 10 percent increase in the cost of carbon-emitting generation under a full pass-through scenario. On average, production falls and unemployment rises, but both responses are small. The response of production depends on the spare capacity of nuclear generation. When nuclear utilization is high, production is 0.36 percentage points lower after 7 months. When it is low, generation shifts more toward carbon-free sources and the response of production is insignificant. Across manufacturing industries, production in the high electricity-intensity group is significantly lower than in the low group for ten months. The results are similar with quarterly GDP and consumption, an alternative measure of nuclear capacity, and other groupings of industries. The cost of carbon pricing will therefore depend on how much nuclear capacity is spare, and it will fall first on electricity-intensive industries.

JEL E32, Q41, Q43, Q48, Q54

Working paperNov 2026

Is “Being Green” Rewarded in the Market?An Empirical Investigation of Decarbonization and Stock Returns

with Soh Young In, Ki Young Park, and Ashby Monk

Abstract

We ask whether carbon-efficient firms earn higher stock returns than carbon-inefficient firms in the same industry, and what the difference reflects. A portfolio that buys the firms with the lowest scope 1 emission intensity in each sector and sells those with the highest earns an alpha of more than 0.4 percent per month from July 2009 to December 2024. The CAPM and the Fama-French three, four and five-factor models do not explain it. The alpha moves with earnings news. Carbon-efficient firms report higher standardized unexpected earnings (SUE) than carbon-inefficient firms, and when this gap enters the factor models the alpha falls by close to half. About four fifths of this fall comes from the nine trading days around earnings announcements. The gap in SUE comes mainly from its denominator. The earnings of carbon-efficient firms are less volatile, so the same change in EPS is larger news for them. Carbon efficiency is thus rewarded in the market even among firms in the same industry, and a large part of the reward arrives with news about earnings that rise more steadily. Why carbon efficiency goes with steady earnings remains open, but it helps to identify firms with stable and growing earnings ahead of their announcements.

Working paperNov 2026

Does Removing the Environmental Bias Reduce Carbon?Evidence from Korea–China Steel Trade

with Jaerim Choi

Abstract

In most countries tariffs are lower on dirty goods than on clean goods, and models suggest that removing this environmental bias of trade policy would lower global emissions. However, a free trade agreement can only cut tariffs. Korea’s tariffs on most dirty upstream steel products were already zero before the Korea–China Free Trade Agreement, so its cuts fall almost only on clean downstream products. The agreement is thus a well-suited setting to study how the carbon embodied in trade responds when downstream tariffs are cut. The tariff cuts reduce the environmental bias in the tariffs of both countries as they are phased in. With a Poisson pseudo-maximum-likelihood regression on monthly trade by product, we find that the carbon embodied in Korea’s imports of the liberalized products rises by 40 percent relative to products whose tariff did not change. The estimate for exports is also positive but it is insignificant. The results hold in four robustness checks. On the products that both countries liberalized, we find suggestive evidence that embodied carbon moved on net into Korea, the cleaner producer of the two. These results imply that removing the bias by cutting downstream tariffs can raise the carbon embodied in trade.

JEL F13, F14, F18, Q54, Q56
